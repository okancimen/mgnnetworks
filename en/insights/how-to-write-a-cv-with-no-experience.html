How to Write a CV With No Experience (Student Template)

1. Why Your First CV Feels Impossible — and Why It Isn't

Opening a blank document and trying to write a CV when you have never had a job is one of the most discouraging experiences in early career life. Every template you find online assumes you have job titles, company names, and a list of responsibilities to slot into neat boxes. You have none of that. So you stare at the page, write your name, and stop.

Here is what the templates do not tell you: hiring managers and programme coordinators who review student applications are not expecting a work history. They know you are 16, 17, or 18. They know you have been in school. What they are looking for is entirely different from what they look for in a candidate with five years of experience. They want evidence of motivation, capability, and self-awareness — and all three of those things can be demonstrated without a single employment entry.

The blank-page problem is really a framing problem. You are not writing a record of jobs you have held. You are writing a portrait of who you are professionally, based on everything you have done so far — and you have done more than you think.

This guide will walk you through every section of a student CV, explain what belongs there, and show you how to write it so it actually reads well to the people who matter.

2. What a Student CV Should Include (and Shouldn't)

Before you open a document, you need to understand what a student CV is and what it is not.

A student CV is a one-page document — one page, not two — that communicates your education, your transferable skills, and any relevant activities or projects you have been involved in. It is not an exhaustive life history. It is not a list of every club you ever attended. It is a curated, honest, well-structured argument that you are worth speaking to.

Include these things

Your full name and contact details — including a professional email address and, if relevant, a LinkedIn profile or portfolio link. Your education section, with your school, the qualifications you are working towards or have achieved, and any notable academic results. A personal statement of three to five sentences. A skills section listing practical and transferable skills you can genuinely demonstrate. Any relevant projects, activities, or voluntary work where you had a real role and produced a real output. Any awards, recognitions, or achievements worth noting.

Leave these out

A photograph (in most countries, including the UK and Singapore, it is standard to omit photos). A line that says "References available upon request" — this is filler. Your date of birth, unless specifically required. Hobbies listed without any context or connection to what you are applying for. Club memberships where you were purely a passive member with no output or responsibility.

The one-page rule: If your CV is running over one page, you have not cut enough. Students with no professional experience should not have a two-page CV. Every line must earn its place.

3. The Four Sections That Matter Most With No Experience

When you have no work history, four sections carry the entire weight of your application. Understanding what each one needs to do — and how to write it well — is most of the battle.

Personal statement / objective

The personal statement is the first thing a hiring manager or programme coordinator reads, and it is the section most students write worst. Most students either copy a generic template ("I am a motivated and hardworking individual seeking an opportunity...") or write something so vague it communicates nothing at all.

A strong personal statement does three things in three to five sentences. It names who you are and where you are in your education. It identifies one or two specific interests or areas of capability that are relevant to what you are applying for. And it explains, directly and honestly, what you are hoping to get from this opportunity and what you are prepared to bring to it.

Here is the difference. A weak version: "I am a hardworking A-level student with a passion for business. I am eager to learn and develop my skills in a professional environment." A strong version: "I am currently in my second year of A-levels, studying Economics, Maths, and Geography. I have been researching sustainable supply chains independently for the past six months and have run a small student-led initiative that reduced our school's single-use plastic consumption by 40%. I am looking for a structured programme where I can apply analytical thinking to a real business problem and receive feedback from professionals working in the field."

The second version is specific. It contains a result. It shows independent initiative. It explains what the candidate wants and offers something in return. Write your personal statement last, after you have filled in the rest of the CV — you will know what to say once you can see everything else laid out.

Education

Your education section is your primary credential right now. Give it the space it deserves. List your current school or college, the years you attended, and the qualifications you are working towards. If you have results — predicted grades, completed exams, module scores — include them. If you are a top performer in one or two subjects particularly relevant to the role, call that out explicitly: "Top of year in Economics" or "Distinction in Design Technology" tells the reader something. "Studying A-levels" tells them almost nothing.

If you have completed any online courses, certifications, or workshops that are directly relevant, include them at the bottom of this section — but only if they are from a credible source and ideally paired with a project that demonstrates the skill. A certificate alone is weak. A certificate plus a project that shows what you did with the knowledge is meaningful.

Skills

The skills section trips up most students because they either list things so generic they mean nothing ("communication," "teamwork," "leadership") or they include skills they cannot actually demonstrate under questioning.

The rule is simple: only list skills you could talk about for two minutes if someone asked you to give an example. If you list "data analysis," you should be ready to describe a specific moment when you analysed data, what tool you used, and what you concluded. If you list "project management," you should have a project you managed, with a timeline and an outcome.

Group your skills into two or three categories if you have enough. Technical skills — software you can use, languages you speak, tools you have worked with — are more credible than soft skills listed in isolation. If you can code in Python, speak French fluently, or use Adobe Illustrator professionally, those are concrete and checkable. Lead with specifics, and let soft skills appear through your examples rather than as standalone bullet points.

Projects and activities

This is the section that replaces a work history, and it is where most students undersell themselves by either leaving it empty or describing what they did without saying what happened as a result.

A project can be almost anything: a school extended essay or independent study, an app or website you built, a community initiative you organised, a business you started (even a small one), a creative project you completed and published, a fundraising campaign, or a volunteer role where you had genuine responsibility. The question is not what it was — it is whether you can describe it in terms of a task, an action, and an outcome.

For each entry, follow this structure: what was the project or activity, what was your specific role, what did you do, and what was the result. Keep each entry to two or three bullet points. Use numbers wherever you can. "Organised an event for 80 students" is better than "organised an event." "Wrote 12 articles published on the school website" is better than "wrote for the school website."

4. How to Write Strong Bullet Points When You Have Nothing

The phrase "I have nothing to put on my CV" is almost never literally true. What it usually means is: "I have not yet translated what I have done into the language CVs use." Bullet points on a CV follow a specific structure, and once you understand it, you can apply it to almost any experience.

The standard formula is: action verb + task + outcome. Start every bullet point with a verb in the past tense (led, organised, created, researched, built, managed, wrote, designed, coordinated, analysed). Then describe what you did. Then add the result, wherever possible.

Examples of weak bullet points and their stronger versions:

  • Weak: "Member of school debate team." Strong: "Competed in regional debate tournament representing the school, reaching the quarter-finals against 24 teams."
  • Weak: "Helped organise school events." Strong: "Coordinated logistics for the annual school open day, managing a volunteer team of 12 and coordinating arrival of 300 prospective students and parents."
  • Weak: "Interested in coding." Strong: "Built a personal finance tracker in Python as a self-directed project; deployed it on GitHub with full documentation."
  • Weak: "Good with social media." Strong: "Managed social media accounts for a local charity event over six weeks, growing follower count from 120 to 640 and increasing ticket sales by 35%."

The transformation in each case is the same: move from a description of identity or interest to a description of specific action and measurable result. Every bullet point you write should pass this test: could a stranger reading this confirm that you did something real, and that it had a real outcome? If yes, it is a good bullet point. If not, rewrite it.

5. What to Do If You Genuinely Have Nothing Yet

If you have read this far and you genuinely cannot identify a single project, activity, or role to put in the experience section — that is information you need to act on, not a permanent state.

The most direct route is a structured programme. A fellowship or structured internship designed for students gives you something real to do, a real professional to report to, and formal documentation of your contribution when you finish. Unlike a passive work experience placement, a structured programme produces deliverables you can describe on a CV and a reference you can cite. This is the fastest way to go from a blank experience section to a meaningful one.

If you cannot access a structured programme immediately, start smaller. Identify one skill you have or want to develop. Find one person or organisation that could benefit from it. Propose a specific, time-limited project. "Could I spend four weeks redesigning your charity's social media presence, with a goal of increasing engagement by 20%?" is a complete project proposal. If they say yes, you have a project. If they say no, find the next person.

Alternatively, build something independently. Create a blog and publish ten posts. Build an app and document it. Organise a community event and measure the turnout. Run a fundraiser and report the total raised. The activity itself matters less than whether it is real, completed, and has a result you can point to.

6. Six CV Mistakes That Instantly Disqualify Students

Hiring managers and fellowship coordinators who review hundreds of student CVs develop pattern recognition quickly. These are the six mistakes that move a CV directly to the reject pile.

Using a generic template without editing it

A CV generated from an online template with placeholder text barely changed is recognisable immediately. The fonts are default, the section headings are identical to thousands of other CVs, and the content reads like it was written for no one in particular. Templates are a starting point, not a finished product. Every line must be your own.

Describing yourself instead of your actions

Lines like "I am a passionate and dedicated student with excellent communication skills" are adjective claims, not evidence. Anyone can write them. Hiring managers have learned to ignore them entirely. Replace every adjective claim with a concrete example. Instead of "I have strong leadership skills," write: "Led a team of six volunteers to organise a 200-person community fundraiser that raised £3,400."

Misspellings, inconsistent formatting, and grammar errors

This one requires no nuance. A CV with a typo in the first paragraph demonstrates that the candidate did not proofread their most important document. Read your CV aloud before you submit it. Then give it to someone else to read. Inconsistent formatting — different fonts, random capitalisation, uneven spacing — creates the same impression: carelessness.

Including irrelevant or embarrassing content

Listing every GCSE subject with its grade, including a primary school achievement from ten years ago, or mentioning hobbies that have no possible connection to the role all signal that the candidate has not thought critically about what belongs on the document. Every item on your CV should be there for a reason. If you cannot explain why it is there in one sentence, remove it.

Making the CV more than one page

A student with no professional experience who submits a two-page CV either has genuinely extraordinary achievements (rare) or has failed to edit (common). The second is the default assumption. Unless you have won a national award, published research, or founded a company with traction, one page is the right length.

Sending the same CV to every application

Generic CVs perform worse than tailored ones, even when the content is broadly similar. Before submitting, reread the role description and adjust your personal statement and the order of your skills to match what the specific opportunity is looking for. This takes ten minutes and significantly improves your success rate.

7. A Clean Template Structure

Here is a prose walkthrough of how a strong student CV with no work experience should be laid out, from top to bottom.

Header: Your full name in large, clear text at the top. Below it, on one line: your email address, your phone number, and optionally a link to your LinkedIn profile or portfolio. If you have a portfolio or a GitHub with real projects, include it — that link does significant work.

Personal statement: Three to five sentences immediately below the header. No heading needed, or use "Profile" if you prefer. Written in first person. Specific, honest, forward-looking. This is your opening argument.

Education: Listed in reverse chronological order — most recent first. School name, location, dates, qualifications, and results. If you have a compelling academic achievement (highest grade in a subject, academic prize, selection for a selective programme), include it here.

Skills: A short, scannable section. Group by category if you have more than six items. Technical skills first, then language skills, then any other specific capabilities. Only include things you can discuss in an interview.

Projects and activities: This replaces the work experience section. Two to four entries. For each: title, brief context (one line), and two to three bullet points in action-verb format with outcomes. Lead with your most impressive or most relevant entry.

Awards and achievements (optional): If you have a national, regional, or significant institutional award, include it here. If you do not, omit the section entirely rather than padding it with minor prizes.

That is the entire structure. Keep it clean, keep it specific, keep it to one page. A CV that is easy to read and contains genuine evidence of capability will consistently outperform a longer, more visually elaborate document that is thin on actual content.
