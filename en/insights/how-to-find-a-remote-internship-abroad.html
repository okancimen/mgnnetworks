How to Find a Remote Internship Abroad — Guide for High School Graduates (2026)

Short answer: You do not need to be enrolled in university to do a remote internship abroad. Research, content, data, and operations support roles are regularly open to recent high school graduates — LinkedIn, AIESEC, and direct company career pages are the three most reliable channels.

1. Why Doing a Remote Internship Before University Matters

Most students arrive at university with identical CVs: good grades, a few school clubs, possibly some volunteer work. By the time they reach their final year — when graduate recruiters start paying attention — the candidates who stand out are almost always those who built something before they arrived. A remote international internship, done in the gap between high school and university, is one of the most effective ways to create a CV that looks fundamentally different from every other applicant's.

The case is not primarily about the work itself, though that matters. It is about proof of initiative. Getting a remote internship with an international company — without a degree, without an institutional introduction, by writing a cold application in a second language — is a meaningful act that most 18-year-olds never attempt. Universities and employers both notice when someone has done it.

There is also a practical timing argument. The window between finishing high school and starting university — typically three to eight months — is one of the last genuinely unstructured periods you will have for years. Remote internships can be done from anywhere, with no relocation cost, and scheduled around travel or other commitments. The barrier is lower than most people assume.

2. Roles That Don't Require a Degree

A common misconception is that remote internships are only available to enrolled university students. This is simply not true. Many of the most accessible entry points are functions where drive and basic skills matter more than credentials — and these functions happen to be exactly where most remote internships exist.

Social media and content

Startups and marketing agencies hire junior content support constantly. Typical tasks include drafting post copy, creating content calendars, researching hashtags, and scheduling posts via tools like Buffer or Later. If you can demonstrate a basic understanding of what works on Instagram, LinkedIn, or TikTok — even through a personal account — you are employable for this role.

Research and data

Companies across industries — consulting firms, market research agencies, tech startups — regularly need people to gather, clean, and format data. No degree required. Required: attention to detail, a spreadsheet competence, and the ability to follow a brief. These roles are often posted as short-term projects (4–8 weeks) and are ideal first internships.

Junior web and app development

If you can write HTML and CSS, or build basic functionality in Python or JavaScript, you are ahead of a significant portion of professional candidates for junior tech support roles. Many small tech companies post remote internships specifically looking for junior developers who will work under supervision — no computer science degree required, working code required.

Graphic design and visual production

Canva-level skills get you through the door for most marketing support roles. If you have worked with Figma, Adobe Illustrator, or Premiere Pro, you are competitive for design internships at small agencies and startups who cannot afford to hire a full-time designer.

Administrative and operations support

The least glamorous option, but consistently available: scheduling, inbox management, CRM data entry, meeting notes. These roles teach you how professional organisations actually function — a surprisingly valuable foundation — and they frequently convert to something more substantive once you prove yourself reliable.

The pattern across all these roles: companies post remote internships for high school graduates when they have real work to get done, a small budget, and no time to run a formal graduate recruitment process. The person who gets the role is not the most qualified — it is the one who applies first, writes well, and demonstrates they understand what the company actually does.

3. What English Level Do You Need?

For roles at international English-language companies, B2 (upper-intermediate) is the practical minimum for most written communication roles — content, research, admin. At this level you can write clear, comprehensible emails and documents, follow complex instructions, and participate in video calls without being a barrier to the meeting.

C1 (advanced) opens the full range of roles, including client-facing communication, more complex research, and anything that requires nuanced writing or editing.

For technical roles — development, data, design — the English requirement is significantly lower. If your code runs and your design files are well-labelled, B1 (intermediate) is usually sufficient to collaborate effectively on a small remote team.

You do not need a formal certificate to demonstrate your English level in a remote internship application. A well-written cover letter is itself the evidence. If you have IELTS, TOEFL, or a school-leaving exam grade that demonstrates B2 or above, include it. If you do not, focus on making your application documents flawless — a single typo or grammatical error will cost you the interview.

4. Where to Find Remote International Internships

The platforms below are where the majority of accessible remote internships are posted. Use multiple simultaneously — the same opportunity rarely appears on more than one or two platforms, and the highest-quality roles are posted and filled quickly.

LinkedIn

The most important platform for professional internships globally. Search "remote internship" filtered by "Entry level" and your target function. Set up a job alert so you are notified within hours of posting. Apply within 24 hours of a listing going live — response rates drop sharply after the first few days. Your LinkedIn profile should be complete and professional before you start applying: headline, summary, and any projects or courses you have completed.

AIESEC

AIESEC is a global youth-led organisation with chapters in over 100 countries that connects young people with international internship placements. Many placements are project-based and remote. AIESEC internships typically include mentorship and are structured for students and recent graduates with little prior experience. Apply through the AIESEC website or through your national chapter — the application process is free in most countries.

We Work Remotely and Remote.co

These are specialist remote job boards with a consistent supply of internship listings, particularly for tech, marketing, and operations roles. Filter for "Intern" in the job type. Quality of listings varies — focus on companies with an identifiable online presence and a clear description of what the internship involves.

Internshala

Originally an Indian internship platform, Internshala now lists remote internships from companies across Southeast Asia, South Asia, and increasingly the Middle East and Eastern Europe. It is particularly accessible to applicants without prior work experience, and many listings explicitly state "freshers welcome." Free to use; create a profile and apply directly on the platform.

Company career pages

Direct applications to company career pages are underused and often more successful than platform applications. Identify 10–20 companies in your target industry whose work you find interesting, check their careers page weekly, and apply the moment a relevant internship is posted. For smaller companies, a speculative application — a direct email to the hiring manager explaining what you can offer — occasionally works better than waiting for a formal listing.

Opportunity Desk and YouthsToday

These aggregators collect internships, fellowships, and exchange programmes from organisations worldwide, with a strong representation of NGOs, international organisations, and development-focused companies. Useful if you are interested in public sector, non-profit, or social impact work.

5. Writing Your Application With No Experience

The challenge of applying for your first remote internship is that you are asking someone to take a chance on you with no professional track record. The solution is to replace the missing track record with two things: demonstrable interest in what the company does, and a concrete signal that you can do the specific work they need.

Your CV

Keep it to one page. Include: your most recent qualification, any relevant coursework, tools and software you know (even at a basic level), languages, and any projects — school or personal — that show relevant capability. If you have an online portfolio, GitHub profile, or Behance page, include the link prominently. Do not pad with generic phrases. A CV that honestly shows limited experience but is well-designed and clearly written beats a padded CV every time.

Your cover letter

Three paragraphs, no longer. First: why you are interested in this specific company (not internships in general — this specific company). Second: the one or two things you can actually do that are relevant to the role. Third: what you are hoping to learn and your availability. The cover letter should contain no clichés. It should read as if a specific, curious person wrote it about a specific company they actually researched.

Before you apply: spend 15 minutes reading the company's recent blog posts, social media, and About page. Reference something specific from that research in your cover letter. It takes three minutes to tell whether an applicant has actually looked at your company — most have not, and those who have are immediately distinguishable.

A work sample

For most roles, including one unsolicited work sample dramatically increases your callback rate. For a content role: a mock social post or article in the company's voice. For a research role: a one-page research brief on a topic they have recently covered. For a development role: a link to a project on GitHub. It does not have to be perfect — it has to show that you understand the work and took the initiative to try it.

6. The Interview Process

Remote internship interviews at smaller companies are usually informal: a 20–30 minute video call with one or two people, focused on fit and competence rather than formal assessment. At larger companies or more competitive programmes, there may be a written task or technical test before or after the call.

Video call preparation

Test your audio, camera, and internet connection before the interview. Use a neutral, well-lit background. Arrive on the call a minute early. Have the job description and your CV open in a tab you can reference if needed. Prepare three to five questions to ask at the end — asking nothing signals disinterest.

Common questions

You will almost certainly be asked why you want to intern with this specific company, what you hope to learn, and how you have handled a challenge in the past. The challenge question is tricky for first-time applicants — a school project, an independent learning experience, or even a personal situation works fine. What matters is the structure of your answer: situation, what you did, and what the outcome was.

Time zones

If the company is in a different time zone, address it proactively. Mention your time zone at the start of the interview and confirm the expected working hours. Most remote internships for pre-university students involve asynchronous work — you deliver tasks by a deadline rather than working live hours — which makes time zones far less of a constraint than they appear.

7. Getting Paid Across Borders

Remote internship stipends vary widely — from $0 (unpaid) to $400–800 USD per month for more structured programmes at professional companies. For paid positions, you will typically need to provide banking details to receive an international transfer.

Wise (formerly TransferWise)

The most practical solution for receiving international payments as a student. Wise gives you local bank details in multiple currencies (GBP, EUR, USD, AUD) that you share with your employer. They transfer in their currency; Wise converts at the mid-market rate and deposits in your local currency. Setup takes about 10 minutes and requires a government-issued ID. Fees are substantially lower than traditional bank international wire transfers.

PayPal

Widely used for small, project-based payments from international clients. Adequate for monthly stipends up to around $500 — for larger amounts, fees become meaningful. Currency conversion rates are less favourable than Wise, but setup is simpler and the platform is universally accepted.

Unpaid internships

Unpaid internships are worth considering under three conditions: the company provides structured mentorship, the work is genuinely interesting and builds your skills, and you receive a formal written reference at the end. If a company cannot commit to a written reference, the unpaid arrangement has no long-term value to you. Always clarify the reference expectation before you start.

8. Turning a Remote Internship Into a CV Asset

The internship itself is only half the asset. The other half is what you do with it on your CV, in university applications, and when asking for a reference.

Document your work in real time

Keep a brief weekly record of what you worked on, what you delivered, and any feedback you received. This becomes the raw material for your CV entry and for briefing your reference writer. Without notes, most people cannot remember the specifics of work done three months ago — and vague specifics produce vague references.

Your CV entry

A strong internship entry reads: role, company name, dates, and two to three bullet points of what you actually delivered — not what your responsibilities were. "Wrote and scheduled 24 social media posts generating 12% above-average engagement" beats "Responsible for social media content" every time. Quantify wherever possible. If you cannot quantify, describe the output: "Produced a 15-page competitive analysis of five market entrants used in the company's Q4 strategy presentation."

Your reference

At the halfway point of your internship, ask your supervisor directly whether they would be willing to write a reference when the placement concludes. If yes, brief them in writing at the end: remind them of the specific projects you worked on, the outcomes, and what you would like them to highlight. A reference written from your own brief is almost always more specific — and therefore more credible — than one written from memory.
