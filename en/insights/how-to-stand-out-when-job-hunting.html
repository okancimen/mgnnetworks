How to Stand Out When Job Hunting

TL;DR: Most applications are filtered out before a human reads them. The candidates who get interviews combine a tailored CV, a personalized cover letter, an active LinkedIn, and at least one piece of documented proof — a project, certificate, or structured internship. This article gives you 10 specific strategies.

The job market rewards preparation. Here is exactly what that preparation looks like — for students and graduates applying with limited experience.

The average job posting at an entry-level role receives over 250 applications. Most are filtered by an Applicant Tracking System before any human sees them. Of the ones that reach a recruiter, the majority are screened in under 10 seconds. Standing out is not about luck — it is about understanding what gets filtered and what gets noticed, then acting on that knowledge consistently.

This article covers 10 strategies that work specifically for students and recent graduates — people who have limited formal experience but need to build a credible case that they are worth interviewing. Each strategy is actionable today.

1. Tailor Your CV to Each Role

Sending the same CV to every job is the single most common reason strong candidates get rejected before interview. ATS systems scan for keywords from the job description — if your CV does not mirror that language, it is filtered out automatically. Hiring managers who do read CVs are comparing you against the specific requirements listed in the posting.

The fix is simple but time-consuming: for every application, re-read the job description and adjust your CV's summary line, top skills, and bullet points to reflect the exact phrasing used. You do not need to fabricate anything — you need to describe what you have done in the same language the employer uses to describe what they need. A CV tailored this way takes 15 extra minutes and significantly increases the chance a human reviews it.

Quick action: Copy the job description into a word cloud tool. The largest words are your priority keywords. Check whether your CV includes them. If not, find truthful ways to incorporate them before applying.

2. Write a Personalized Cover Letter

Hiring managers can identify a template cover letter in the first two lines. Phrases like "I am writing to apply for the position of..." or "I believe my skills make me a strong candidate..." signal that the applicant has not invested time in the application. That signal costs you the interview.

A personalized cover letter references something specific: a recent product launch, a company initiative, a challenge in the industry the company is facing, or something you genuinely admire about how they operate. It then connects that observation to a specific thing you have done. One specific paragraph built on research outperforms three generic paragraphs built on templates — every time.

3. Build a Strong LinkedIn Profile

Over 87% of recruiters use LinkedIn to source and screen candidates. A weak or incomplete profile means you are invisible to the majority of people who could hire you. A strong profile means you are findable before you even apply.

The highest-impact changes: write a headline that describes what you do or what you are building toward (not just "Student at X University"), fill the About section with two to three sentences about what you are looking for and what you bring, and add every project, certificate, volunteer role, and relevant course. Recruiters use keyword search — the more completely your profile is filled, the more searches you appear in.

4. Create a Portfolio of Real Projects

The most credible thing on a CV without formal experience is documented proof of output. A project you built, a case study you wrote, a data analysis you completed, a design you produced — these are evidence of capability, not just claims. Claims are easy to make. Evidence is harder to fake.

You do not need a formal job to build a portfolio. Build a website. Analyze a public dataset. Write a detailed breakdown of a business problem. Contribute to an open-source project. The specific output matters less than the fact that you created something, documented it, and can walk through it in an interview. Even one strong project separates you from the majority of entry-level applicants who have only grades and a list of modules.

5. Earn Free Online Certifications

Google, Meta, Coursera, HubSpot, LinkedIn Learning, and AWS all offer free or low-cost certifications in high-demand skill areas. Completing and listing a relevant certification does two things: it fills a gap in your skill profile, and it demonstrates initiative — a quality every employer values in entry-level candidates because it predicts how the person will behave once hired.

The most useful certifications for general employability in 2026 are in data analysis, digital marketing, project management, and AI tools. Pick the one most relevant to the roles you are targeting. Add it to both your CV and your LinkedIn profile. Do not list a certification that is irrelevant to the role — it adds clutter and signals poor judgment.

6. Apply Through Warm Contacts, Not Just Cold

Research consistently shows that 70–80% of jobs are filled through networks before or instead of public postings. Even for roles that are publicly advertised, an application accompanied by a referral from someone inside the company moves to the top of the pile. Cold applications — sending your CV into a job board with no connection to the company — are the least efficient use of your job-hunting time.

Building warm contacts does not require a large existing network. Tell every person you know that you are looking. Ask professors, former employers, volunteer supervisors, and family friends if they know anyone in the field. Use LinkedIn to find second-degree connections at companies you are targeting and ask for a 15-minute informational conversation. One warm introduction is worth fifty cold applications.

7. Apply Early and Follow Up

Job postings attract a surge of applications in the first 48 hours and then taper off. Shortlisting often happens before the posting closes. Applying on the same day a job is posted puts your application in front of the recruiter before they are fatigued by volume.

Following up matters equally. A short, professional email one week after applying — confirming your application, expressing continued interest, offering to provide anything additional — takes two minutes to write and bumps your name back into active consideration. Most applicants never follow up. Doing so distinguishes you as someone who actually wants the role.

8. Research the Company Before Applying

Candidates who reference specific, accurate knowledge about a company in their cover letter or interview stand out immediately — because the majority of applicants do not do this. Read the company's recent news, latest annual report or investor update, LinkedIn posts from senior leadership, and reviews on platforms like Glassdoor. Identify a genuine challenge or opportunity they are navigating.

Then use this in your application: one sentence in your cover letter that references something specific and connects it to why you want to work there. In an interview, asking an informed question about a challenge you identified signals preparation, curiosity, and seriousness — the three traits every employer wants at entry level.

9. Practice Interview Answers Before You Need Them

The candidates who perform best in interviews are not the ones who think fastest on their feet. They are the ones who have prepared concrete stories they can adapt to any question. The STAR method — Situation, Task, Action, Result — is the standard framework for behavioral interview questions. Prepare 5–10 stories using this structure before any interview. Practice them out loud until they feel natural, not rehearsed.

Cover the common behavioral themes: a time you solved a problem under pressure, a time you worked in a team and there was conflict, a time you had to learn something quickly, a time you failed and what you learned. These four themes cover the majority of behavioral questions you will face at the entry level.

10. Join a Structured Fellowship or Internship Program

If you are applying with little or no formal experience, the most powerful thing you can do is acquire documented, structured experience as quickly as possible. A structured program gives you three things that self-study and informal experience cannot: a named mentor who is accountable for your development, real deliverables you can show to future employers, and a formal reference letter from a credible organization.

These three elements — mentor, deliverable, reference — are what convert an experience into a credential. Without them, an experience is a claim. With them, it is evidence. If you are starting from zero, joining a fellowship or structured internship is the fastest path to a CV that gets interviews.
