How to Write an English CV for University Applications (2026 Guide)

Short answer: An English-language university CV differs from most national formats: 1–2 pages maximum, no photo, no date of birth, reverse-chronological order, and qualifications translated into the target country's grading system. UK, Dutch, and Australian universities are the most likely to require one.

1. Which Universities Ask for a CV

Not every international university application requires a CV in the traditional sense. The UK's UCAS system uses a personal statement. The US Common App uses an activities list and supplemental essays. These platforms have their own structures that capture much of the same information a CV would contain — just in a different format.

Where a CV is specifically requested: most Australian universities (UAC, VTAC, direct portals), many Canadian institutions, Dutch universities outside numerus fixus programmes, Singapore's NUS and NTU, Hong Kong universities, and most scholarship applications worldwide. Even where it is not formally required, preparing a comprehensive CV is excellent practice — it forces you to inventory your experiences systematically, and the work you do preparing it feeds directly into your personal statement, activities list, and interview preparation.

The other context where a student CV becomes essential: when applying for the structured programmes, fellowships, and internships that generate the professional references and extracurricular evidence that strengthen university applications. The CV is often the first impression you make on a programme admissions team.

2. International CV vs Your National Format — What Changes

Many countries have their own CV conventions — some of which are explicitly not appropriate in an international English-medium context. If you are accustomed to a CV format that includes any of the following, remove them before submitting to any English-medium institution:

  • Photo: Not included in CVs for UK, US, Australian, Canadian, or most other English-medium applications. Including one raises questions, not confidence.
  • Date of birth or age: Not required. The institution knows you are a student applicant.
  • Nationality or passport details: Not relevant unless the application specifically asks for visa status.
  • National identity number: Never include on a CV. This is a security risk and not something any legitimate institution requires.
  • Marital status or family information: Not relevant and not expected.
  • Objective statement that describes what you want: Replace with a brief profile that describes who you are and what you bring.

What replaces these is a focus on evidence: what you have done, what you produced, what responsibilities you held, and what standard your work was held to. The international English CV is a document of output, not biography.

3. How to Describe Your Qualifications in English

This is where many international students make their first significant error — either over-explaining their qualifications (filling half the CV with descriptions of their national education system) or under-explaining (writing a name in their national language that the reader cannot interpret).

The right approach is a middle path: translate the name of your qualification into plain English, add the national name in brackets if it may not be recognised, and list your grade or score. Admissions readers at international universities have country-by-country equivalency tables and are familiar with major national qualifications. You do not need to explain what the IB is. You may need to briefly clarify a less internationally known qualification.

For qualifications that are very widely known internationally — IB Diploma, A-Levels, German Abitur — you can use the name directly with grade. For qualifications that are primarily national — the French Bac, Spanish Selectividad, Chinese Gaokao, Russian EGE, university entrance exams in various countries — a brief English description alongside the national name is the right balance.

4. CV Structure Section by Section

A student CV for university applications should be one to two pages. For most school-age applicants it will be one page. Two pages is acceptable if you have genuinely substantive things to say in each section — it should not be two pages because you have padded content to fill space.

Contact information

Name, city and country, email address, phone number with international dialling code. If you have a LinkedIn profile, a portfolio website, or a GitHub (for technical programmes), include the URL. No photo, no date of birth, no identity numbers.

Profile (2–3 sentences)

A concise summary of who you are as a student applicant. Not "I am a motivated student who wants to study at your excellent university." That says nothing. Something like: "Year 12 student with a strong record in Mathematics and Economics, one year of structured professional project experience through the Magenta Networks Global Career Fellowship, and a sustained commitment to competitive debate. Applying to study Economics." This tells the reader your subject area, your strongest credentials, and what distinguishes you — in three sentences.

Education

List your most recent and current qualification first. Include institution name, qualification name (in English with national name in brackets), dates, and grades. Include any relevant academic achievements or distinctions. If you are sitting national exams this year, list your predicted grades or most recent results.

Experience

Any work, structured professional programmes, fellowships, internships, or significant volunteering. For each entry: organisation, your role, dates, and two to three bullet points describing what you actually did — not duties, but outputs and responsibilities. Use action verbs: produced, led, designed, analysed, coordinated, delivered.

Activities and leadership

Clubs, sports, competitions, school roles. Keep this section tight — two to five entries that show depth and progression, not every club you have ever attended. For each entry, name it and describe your role and contribution concretely.

Skills

Language proficiency (with test scores if applicable), software, technical skills. Be honest — "intermediate Python" is better than "Python" if you are not at a professional level. Include your language test score (IELTS, TOEFL, DELF, etc.) and date here if you have one.

5. Extracurricular Activities — From Weak to Strong Entries

The extracurricular section is where most student CVs underperform — not because students lack activities, but because they describe them in the weakest possible way. Listing a club you belong to, without describing what you did or produced, tells the reader almost nothing. Here is what the difference looks like:

Weak entry
Model United Nations Club — Member (2024–present)
Participated in MUN conferences and represented countries in committee debates.
Strong entry
Model United Nations, National High School Circuit — Chair, Security Council Committee (2025–2026)
Chaired a 40-delegate committee at the national MUN conference; co-wrote the procedural handbook adopted by four subsequent conferences. Best Delegate, Regional Championship, 2025.
Weak entry
School Football Team — Player
Played for the school football team for two years.
Strong entry
School Football Team — Captain (2025–2026), Player (2023–2026)
Led team of 18 to regional semi-final in 2026, first time school reached this stage in seven years. Coordinated training schedule with two assistant coaches and organised pre-match analysis sessions.

The pattern in both strong entries is the same: a specific role or outcome, a specific achievement or output, and a number or comparison that makes the achievement concrete. Titles alone ("Captain," "Chair") are weak without the supporting evidence of what you did with the role.

6. What to Do If You Have No Work Experience

Most school-age students have limited formal work experience. University admissions readers know this and do not expect a full professional career history. What they do expect — and what distinguishes competitive applicants — is evidence that you have done something beyond attending school.

If your Experience section is empty, that is a signal to build something before you apply. The most accessible routes for school-age students:

  • Structured fellowship programmes: Designed specifically for students with no prior experience, these provide a defined programme, real work, professional feedback, and a reference on completion. The Magenta Networks Global Career Fellowship is open to students aged 14–22 with no prior experience required.
  • Part-time or seasonal employment: Any legitimate paid job creates a potential employer reference and demonstrates professional conduct under real conditions.
  • Volunteering with real responsibilities: Helping at an event once is not CV material. A six-month role with specific tasks, regular hours, and a named supervisor who can speak to your performance is.
  • Academic competitions with tangible outputs: Science Olympiad, national essay prizes, robotics competitions — these go in the Activities section and demonstrate initiative and skill beyond coursework.

The right time to start is now. A fellowship or work programme started in Year 10 gives you one to two years of track record before university applications. Started in Year 12, the application deadline may have already passed before the programme ends. The earlier you act, the more options you have.

7. Common Mistakes That Weaken Student CVs

These are the errors that appear most frequently in student CVs submitted to international applications:

  • Generic profile statements. "I am a hardworking and motivated student" is invisible. Every student who submits a CV says something similar. Replace character claims with specific evidence of who you are.
  • Activities described as attendance, not contribution. "Member of the debate club" vs "Competed in 12 regional competitions, reached national final, trained six junior members." The first is noise. The second is evidence.
  • Skill lists without evidence. "Leadership, communication, teamwork" listed under Skills with no context. These words are meaningless without the experiences that demonstrate them. Move skills evidence into your activity and experience descriptions.
  • Including irrelevant biographical information. Photo, date of birth, national ID number, marital status — none of these belong on an international university CV.
  • Formatting that obscures content. Dense text, inconsistent fonts, decorative elements, charts of "skill levels" — these distract from content. A clean, readable, single-font document is more impressive than a designed one that is hard to scan.
  • Qualification names that mean nothing to the reader. Translate your qualification into English and add its national name in brackets. Do not assume the reader knows what your national exam abbreviation stands for.
