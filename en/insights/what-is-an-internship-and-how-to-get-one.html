1. What an Internship Actually Is

Most students have a rough sense that an internship is some kind of work experience — you go to a company, you help out, and at the end you put it on your CV. That is true in outline but misleading in practice, because it misses the specific thing that makes an internship valuable: the combination of real work, real accountability, and real professional context.

An internship is a fixed-term placement inside an organisation where you work as a junior contributor on real tasks that matter to the business. You are not there to shadow. You are not there to observe. You are there to contribute — and the quality of your contribution is assessed against something resembling a professional standard, even if the bar is adjusted for your experience level.

The duration varies. Some internships last two weeks. Some last six months. The most valuable ones — in terms of what you learn and what you can prove — tend to run for at least four to six weeks, long enough for you to actually produce something meaningful rather than spending most of your time getting orientated.

Pay varies too. In some countries, internships are required by law to pay at least the minimum wage. In others, unpaid internships remain common, particularly in sectors like media, fashion, and non-profits. Whether paid or unpaid, the professional credential — the experience on your CV, the reference letter you earn — is what most students are ultimately after.

The key question to ask about any internship: Will I produce something real that a named professional can verify and describe in writing? If the answer is no, the experience may not carry the weight you expect.

2. How Internships Differ from Fellowships and Work Placements

Students frequently use "internship," "fellowship," and "work placement" interchangeably. They are not the same thing, and understanding the distinctions helps you choose the right opportunity for your stage.

Internship

A placement inside a company where you work on real tasks as a junior contributor. The structure varies enormously — some internships have defined learning objectives, regular feedback, and formal check-ins; others are closer to temporary employment with minimal supervision. An internship's value depends heavily on the organisation, the manager you are assigned to, and whether the work you are given is genuinely meaningful. There is no guarantee of documentation at the end.

Fellowship

A fellowship is a structured programme with a defined curriculum, explicit learning objectives, and formal assessment. Fellowships are designed around the participant's development — they are built to produce a specific outcome, often a reference letter or certificate that describes exactly what you contributed and what standard your work reached. Fellowships are typically more selective than internships and often more accessible to younger students, since they are explicitly designed for emerging professionals rather than people already in higher education.

Work placement

A work placement is usually a longer-term arrangement — often part of a formal university degree — where you spend a semester or a year working inside an organisation as part of your academic programme. The placement is co-managed by your university and the employer, and it typically carries academic credit. Work placements are generally not available to students who are not already enrolled in a relevant degree.

For most secondary school students and early-stage university students, a fellowship is often a better bet than a traditional internship. It is designed for you, it produces guaranteed documentation, and it is assessed against a professional standard from the start.

3. What Interns Actually Do Day-to-Day

The honest answer is: it depends entirely on the organisation and the manager. The range is wide.

At the best internships, you will be given a defined project at the start, work on it with real supervision, and present your output at the end. You will sit in on meetings where decisions are made. You will receive feedback on your work. You will understand, by the end, how this particular organisation operates and where your contribution fit.

At average internships, you will spend a significant portion of your time doing administrative or logistical tasks — booking rooms, formatting documents, handling correspondence — with occasional opportunities to contribute to more substantive work when someone is too busy to do it themselves.

At poor internships, you will make coffee, watch people work, and leave with nothing documentable.

Before accepting an internship, it is worth asking directly: what specific project or output will I be responsible for? If the answer is vague, that is a signal about the quality of the placement.

Tasks interns commonly handle

  • Conducting research and summarising findings for the team
  • Writing first drafts of documents, reports, or communications
  • Managing or contributing to a defined project with a deadline
  • Supporting client-facing work under supervision
  • Analysing data and producing summaries or recommendations
  • Building or maintaining content — social media, websites, materials

4. The Realistic Requirements to Land One as a Student

The barrier to a first internship is lower than most students assume — particularly for roles aimed at undergraduates and recent school-leavers. Organisations offering internships to students do not expect a track record. What they do expect is a combination of things you can develop and demonstrate without any prior experience.

Genuine interest in the field

The most common reason first internship applications fail is that they are generic. The applicant has not demonstrated any specific interest in what this organisation does. Hiring managers for internship roles read dozens of applications from students who want "to gain experience in business" — a phrase that communicates nothing. Applications that stand out are those where the student clearly understands what the organisation does and has a specific reason for wanting to be there.

Basic professional communication

You do not need to have worked in a professional environment before. You do need to be able to write an email that is clear, respectful, and correctly punctuated. This sounds obvious, but a significant number of student internship applications are undermined by poor writing — not because the candidate lacks ability, but because they did not treat the application as a professional document.

A relevant skill or interest

Even without prior employment, you likely have skills that are relevant to an internship. If you have done any of the following, they are worth mentioning: built a website, written or edited content, done graphic design work, managed a social media account, tutored someone, conducted research for a school project, organised an event, or contributed to a community initiative. These are real skills. Frame them professionally.

Availability and reliability

Internship managers need to know you will turn up and do what you say you will. Be explicit about your availability, your time zone if the role is remote, and your ability to meet agreed deadlines. Reliability, at the internship stage, is often more important than talent.

5. How to Find Internships

The places most students look first — large corporate internship portals — are not always the best places to find your first internship. They attract hundreds of applications for every position, and they are optimised for students with some prior professional experience. Here is a more practical approach.

Start with your network, not a database

The majority of internship placements — particularly at smaller organisations — are filled through personal connection before they are ever advertised. Tell every adult in your network that you are looking for internship experience in a specific area. Teachers, family contacts, parents of friends, coaches — anyone who has a professional connection to the field you are interested in. You are not asking them to get you a job. You are asking whether they know anyone who might be open to a short placement.

Target small and medium organisations directly

Small and medium-sized businesses are often more open to student internships than large ones, for a simple reason: they have less bureaucracy around hiring. A well-written, specific email to a managing director or founder of a twenty-person company will often receive a response where the same email to a large corporation would not. Research five to ten organisations in your area of interest, identify the right contact, and write them a direct email.

Use LinkedIn strategically

LinkedIn is useful not primarily as a job board but as a research tool. Search for professionals in your target field, look at their career histories, and identify organisations that have previously hosted interns. Reach out directly with a brief, personalised message. Keep it short: who you are, what you are looking for, and why you are contacting them specifically.

Structured programmes and fellowships

If you are under 18 or at the very beginning of your career, look for structured programmes designed specifically for emerging professionals. These are often easier to access than traditional internships because the selection criteria are designed around potential rather than prior experience. 6. How to Write an Internship Application With No Experience

The challenge of the first application is that you are being asked to demonstrate professional value without the professional track record that usually provides that demonstration. The solution is to shift the evidence base — instead of pointing to jobs you have had, you point to qualities and capabilities you can demonstrate through other means.

Write a cover letter that is specific, not generic

The cover letter is the most important part of a student internship application. Its purpose is to answer two questions: why this organisation, and why you. "I am a motivated student looking to gain experience" answers neither. A good cover letter names something specific about the organisation — a project they completed, a value they express, a problem they work on — and connects it to something real and specific about you. Three short paragraphs is enough. Most student cover letters are too long, not too short.

Build a minimal CV that leads with skills

If you have no employment history, do not try to hide this by padding your CV with irrelevant details. Instead, lead with a skills section that names specific things you can do — write, design, research, analyse, manage social media, code — followed by any context that gives those skills credibility: coursework, personal projects, school responsibilities, volunteer work. Keep it to one page. Use clean formatting.

Include any relevant project work

If you have built anything, written anything, organised anything, or contributed to anything that demonstrates a relevant skill, include it. Link to it if it is online. A personal project that shows genuine interest and real output is worth more than an empty employment history section. The work does not need to be impressive in absolute terms — it needs to demonstrate that you have taken initiative and completed something real.

Follow up once

If you have not heard back from an application after two weeks, send one brief follow-up email. Many student applications are delayed or overlooked, and a polite follow-up demonstrates the kind of proactive professionalism that internship managers value. Do not follow up more than once — it signals desperation rather than initiative.

7. What Happens After — Making the Most of It

Getting the internship is not the end of the task. What you do during and after the placement determines whether the experience produces lasting value.

Treat it like a real job

Be on time — or early. Respond to messages within the day. Meet every deadline, and flag any risks to deadlines before they are missed. Ask questions when you need to, but arrive prepared. The habits you establish in your first placement become the habits you carry into every subsequent one. Professionalism, at the internship stage, is the most differentiating quality you can demonstrate.

Ask for specific, actionable feedback

Do not wait until the end of the placement to find out how you are doing. After completing your first project or your first two weeks, ask your supervisor: "Is there anything specific I should do differently?" Most supervisors will give vague positive feedback unless you ask a precise question. Specific feedback is what allows you to improve during the placement rather than after it ends.

Document everything as you go

Keep a record of every project you contribute to: what the brief was, what you did, what the outcome was. You will need this information to update your CV after the placement, and you will be surprised how quickly the specific details fade. A simple document listing dates, tasks, and outcomes is sufficient. This is also the material you will draw on when asking for a reference.

Ask for a reference letter before you leave

Raise the subject of a reference letter early — ideally in the first week. Explain that you are building a professional portfolio and that a written reference from a named supervisor would be very valuable. Most managers will agree when asked early and directly. Asking at the last moment, or after you have left, frequently produces vague or delayed results. The reference letter is the evidence that makes everything else you did during the placement verifiable.

Stay in touch

The professionals you work with during an internship are the beginning of your professional network. Connect with them on LinkedIn after the placement. Send a brief thank-you message when you leave. Check in occasionally with an update about what you have gone on to do. This is not networking in an awkward, transactional sense — it is maintaining a genuine professional relationship that was built on real work. These connections are often how your next opportunity arrives.
