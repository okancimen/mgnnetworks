Does Work Experience Actually Matter for Your Teenager?

The OECD tracked students from age 15 across ten countries and followed them well into their 30s. The findings should change how every parent thinks about their child's school years.

1. What Most Parents Assume — and Why the Data Says Otherwise

Ask most parents what their teenager should be doing between the ages of 15 and 18 and you will hear the same answer: focus on school, get good grades, and worry about work later. It is a reasonable position. Exams are high-stakes, universities are competitive, and the conventional wisdom says that the route to a secure adult life runs through academic results.

The OECD disagrees. Not in a marginal, academic way, but in a measurable, longitudinal, tracked-across-a-working-lifetime way. The research organisation spent years following young people in ten countries from adolescence into their late 20s and early 30s, measuring what actually predicted adult employment, earnings, and wellbeing. The finding that will surprise most parents: teenagers who had structured work experience — part-time jobs, volunteering, formal placements — consistently outperformed their peers across every career metric studied. And the effect was still visible fifteen years later.

This does not mean grades do not matter. It means that grades alone — without any parallel work experience — are a weaker predictor of adult success than most parents and schools assume. The students who do best in the long run are the ones who do both.

The OECD Career Readiness project is one of the most comprehensive longitudinal studies of youth employment ever conducted, drawing on data from Australia, Canada, China, Denmark, Germany, Korea, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Uruguay — plus multiple national cohort studies tracking individuals from adolescence to their early 30s.

2. What the OECD Actually Studied

The OECD Career Readiness project, published in full in 2021, did not rely on surveys or self-reported impressions. It drew on national longitudinal cohort studies — the kind that follow the same individuals for fifteen to twenty years and link education records to employment records, tax data, and welfare outcomes. That means the findings are not about what young people think helped them in retrospect. They are about what actually happened to their earnings, employment rates, and life satisfaction over time.

The ten countries in the primary study were chosen to represent different educational systems, labour markets, and cultural attitudes to youth employment. Australia and Canada have well-established traditions of student part-time work. Germany has a formalised apprenticeship and work placement culture. Denmark integrates workplace learning into its secondary curriculum. The United States, United Kingdom, and others sit in the middle. This diversity makes the findings more robust: the patterns that show up across all ten systems are harder to dismiss as cultural artefacts.

The study looked at three types of youth work experience: part-time employment, volunteering, and formal work placements. It measured outcomes including adult earnings, NEET rates (the proportion of young people not in education, employment, or training — a standard measure of labour market exclusion), job satisfaction, and a broader measure of social and personal confidence called "career readiness."

3. Part-Time Work: The Earnings Premium That Lasts Decades

The headline finding from the part-time work data is striking in its longevity. The OECD did not just find that teenagers who worked part-time got better first jobs. It found that the earnings advantage was still visible — and statistically significant — a decade or more into their working lives.

In Canada, young people who had part-time work experience as teenagers earned on average 5% more at age 30 than those who had not, even after controlling for educational attainment, socioeconomic background, and other confounding variables. In the United Kingdom, the same comparison produced a 6% earnings premium at age 26. These figures are not dramatic individually, but they compound: a 5% earnings advantage maintained across a thirty-year career represents an enormous difference in accumulated wealth.

The employment effects are equally clear. In the United States, teenagers who had worked part-time were 1.28 times less likely to be NEET — not in education, employment, or training — between the ages of 27 and 28, the period when labour market exclusion becomes most costly. That is a 28% lower risk of falling out of the workforce entirely, simply from having had a part-time job as a teenager.

Why does a part-time job at 16 affect earnings at 30? The OECD's explanation centres on what economists call "human capital" — the practical skills and professional behaviours that you cannot learn in a classroom. Showing up on time, taking direction, adapting to feedback, managing competing priorities: these are learned on the job, and they compound. A teenager who develops them at 16 has years of practice by the time they enter the full-time workforce.

There is an important nuance here, and it matters to parents who are worried about academic impact. The positive effects apply to moderate part-time work — roughly 10 to 15 hours per week. The research does not support unlimited hours alongside full-time study. We return to this threshold in section 7.

4. Volunteering: The Numbers Are More Surprising Than You'd Expect

Many parents and teenagers see volunteering as a line on a university application — something you do strategically to fill out a personal statement. The OECD data suggests it is considerably more powerful than that framing implies.

In Australia, teenagers who had volunteered showed an 8% earnings premium at age 26 compared with peers who had not — almost identical to the premium from paid part-time work. In Germany, student volunteers were 41% less likely to be NEET in their early 20s. In Australia again, the effect extended to job satisfaction: volunteers reported meaningfully higher satisfaction scores, independent of their earnings level.

These findings make more sense when you understand what volunteering actually develops, according to the OECD framework. It is not primarily about the organisation you volunteered for. It is about the habits and social capital it builds: taking initiative, operating in a structured environment, building relationships with adults outside the family, and developing a clearer sense of what kind of work you find meaningful. A teenager who has volunteered for two years with a sports club, a food bank, or a community arts project has practised all of these things repeatedly.

There is also a finding in the US data worth noting. The research examined both forced and voluntary volunteers — teenagers who were required to volunteer as part of a school curriculum and those who chose to. A teenager who is initially reluctant to volunteer, but does it consistently, still benefits.

5. Structured Placements: The Strongest Effect of All

If part-time work and volunteering show consistent positive effects, structured work placements — formal programmes where a young person works within an organisation under supervision, with defined objectives and some form of feedback — show the largest effects of all.

In Germany, where work placements are most systematically integrated into secondary education, students who had completed a formal placement were 25% less likely to be NEET in the years following secondary school. In the United Kingdom, a large-scale study found that students with work placements before the age of 16 had 50% lower unemployment rates than comparable peers with no placement experience. Fifty per cent. That is not a marginal statistical difference — it is a transformation of life outcomes.

One of the most actionable findings from the placement data concerns timing and repetition. Students who had work placements both before and after the age of 16 reported the highest levels of benefit: 82% of them said the experience was helpful to their career development. Among those who only had post-16 experience, the figure dropped to 50%. Earlier exposure compounds later exposure. The students who did both were not just more prepared — they were more than proportionally better prepared.

What makes a placement "structured"? The OECD research suggests four elements: a named supervisor who is responsible for the young person's development, defined tasks with some level of challenge, feedback on performance, and some form of documentation — a reference letter, a record of attendance and activities, or a formal review. Placements with these elements consistently outperform informal "come and see what we do" experiences.

6. Three Ways Work Shapes a Young Person's Future

Economists and researchers studying youth employment have converged on a framework that explains why work experience produces such durable effects. It operates through three types of capital: human, social, and cultural. Understanding these helps parents think about which experiences are most valuable for their specific child.

Human capital: skills that transfer

Human capital is the most intuitive: work teaches skills. But the OECD research emphasises that the most valuable skills developed through early work experience are not technical ones. They are what researchers call "non-cognitive skills" or "soft skills" — self-discipline, resilience, time management, the ability to work under direction, the habit of completing tasks even when they are not intrinsically rewarding. These skills are highly transferable across industries and roles, and they are extremely difficult to teach in a classroom. They require practice in real conditions with real consequences.

Social capital: networks that open doors

Social capital refers to the relationships and networks that young people build through work — and particularly the "weak ties" that turn out to matter most for employment. Sociological research has long established that most jobs are found through people you know slightly, not close friends and family. A teenager who has worked in three different settings by the age of 18 has a network of perhaps fifty adults who know them as a capable worker. Those adults are the source of referrals, references, and introductions that no amount of academic achievement can substitute for.

The OECD data shows that students from lower socioeconomic backgrounds benefit disproportionately from structured work placements precisely because they have smaller family networks to draw on. Organised placement programmes essentially democratise access to the kind of weak-tie social capital that more privileged students accumulate naturally through family connections.

Cultural capital: identity and confidence

Cultural capital, in this context, refers to the sense of agency, self-efficacy, and professional identity that young people develop when they see themselves operating successfully in a work environment. A teenager who has had a structured placement — who has been trusted with a task, completed it, and received positive feedback from a real employer — has a fundamentally different relationship to the adult world of work than one who has only heard about it.

This matters because one of the strongest predictors of labour market success is the expectation of success. The OECD data shows that by age 15, 62% of students expect to end up in a professional or managerial role — up from 53% in 2000. But expectation without a realistic path produces what researchers call "occupational aspirations that are out of step with what the labour market can provide." Work experience anchors aspiration to reality, building both confidence and a more accurate map of how the professional world actually works.

7. How Much Is Too Much: The 10–15 Hour Rule

One of the most practically useful findings from the OECD research is the identification of a threshold above which part-time work starts to undermine rather than enhance educational outcomes. That threshold is around 10 to 15 hours per week during term time.

Below this level, the research finds no consistent negative effect on academic performance, and the career benefits described above are clearly present. Above it — particularly above 20 hours per week — academic performance starts to suffer. Students working long hours during term time sleep less, spend less time on homework, and are less likely to pursue further education after secondary school.

This finding has a clear practical implication: the goal is not to maximise hours worked, but to secure consistent, quality experience within a manageable limit. A teenager working 10 hours a week throughout the school year gains more career benefit than one working 30 hours in a single holiday period, both because the consistency builds habits and because it does not compromise study time.

Holidays are different. During school breaks, there is no evidence that working longer hours causes academic harm, and concentrated experience during holidays — a summer programme, a holiday internship, a two-week placement — can be highly valuable. Many of the most productive early work experiences happen in this format precisely because they are intensive without creating a semester-long time conflict.

8. The Aspiration Gap Parents Should Know About

There is one finding in the OECD data that deserves particular attention from parents of teenagers, because it speaks to a risk that is invisible until it is too late to address easily.

The study found a significant and growing gap between the careers that young people aspire to and the range of careers that they are actually aware of. When asked what job they expected to have as adults, the responses of 15-year-olds across the OECD were remarkably narrow: in most countries, just ten to fifteen occupational titles accounted for the majority of answers. The most popular choices clustered around a familiar set — medicine, law, technology, business, sport, entertainment.

This narrowness creates two problems. First, it means many students are chasing a small number of highly competitive careers while ignoring a vast range of equally viable and often more accessible alternatives. Second, it means that students are making major educational decisions based on a very incomplete picture of what the world of work actually contains.

The OECD finding is that students with diverse work experience — across more than one sector, with more than one type of organisation — have significantly broader occupational horizons. They are not just better prepared for the careers they already intend to pursue; they are aware of, and open to, a wider range of possibilities. This is career readiness in the deepest sense: not just the skills to do a specific job, but the informed flexibility to navigate a labour market that will change substantially over a forty-year working life.

9. What Parents Can Do Starting Now

If you are a parent reading this and your teenager is between 13 and 18, the question is not whether work experience matters — the OECD evidence is decisive on that point. The question is where to start and how to make it count.

Start earlier than feels comfortable

Most parents wait until their child is 16 or older before thinking about work experience. The OECD data shows clearly that earlier is better — that experiences before 16 compound with experiences after 16 in ways that produce outsized outcomes. If your child is 14 or 15, this is the right time to begin looking, not in two years' time.

Prioritise structure over prestige

Parents sometimes focus on securing placements at well-known companies, assuming the brand will carry weight. The research suggests this is the wrong priority. What matters is structure: a named supervisor, defined tasks, feedback on performance, and documentation. A placement at a small business where your child is trusted with real work and walks away with a signed reference letter is worth more than a week sitting in on meetings at a large corporation.

Don't overlook volunteering

Volunteering is often treated as a fallback for students who cannot get a paid placement. The OECD data suggests it should be treated as a first-choice option in its own right, especially for younger teenagers. A teenager who volunteers consistently for two years with the same organisation — building relationships with adult supervisors and developing genuine competence — has accumulated significant career capital by the time they start looking for paid work.

Build in breadth over time

Two or three experiences in different sectors, with different types of organisations, produce better outcomes than multiple experiences in exactly the same environment. Breadth of exposure expands occupational awareness and builds a more versatile skillset. It also produces a stronger CV: three placements in three different contexts tells a much more interesting story than three months at the same employer.

Treat documentation as part of the work

An experience only becomes a credential if it can be substantiated. After each placement or volunteering role, make sure your teenager asks for a written reference, keeps a record of what they did and what they learned, and has a contact they can list on an application. The experience without the documentation is like a qualification without a certificate — it happened, but it is much harder to use.

Use school resources before they expire

Many secondary schools have work experience coordinators, partnerships with local businesses, and access to formal placement programmes. These resources disappear the moment your child leaves school. If they are still enrolled, now is the time to use them — not after exams, not next year, now. The school network, particularly the alumni network of students who have already moved into careers, is one of the most underused assets available to a secondary school student.
